Rocket Construction Seminar

Phase III

The final rocket(s)

After our half-success during the second launch, we launched a deep investigation into why the engine/fin mount failed. What we found were bad print settings resulting in a failing component as the pressure escaped to the side rather than the top. This was quickly adjusted, leaving the remaining problem to be bad engines, which is completely out of our control.

To carry the experiments, we planned for a total of three rockets where two would serve as redundancies. Since one rocket was destroyed during the test launch, we had components for two rocket bodies remaining. At this time, the Board MPU was the only technological component left unfinished. The final objectives were building two identical rockets with the updated engine/fin mounts and finishing the Board MPU.

We ended up with one completely black rocket and one painted identically to the first big one - some form of tribute to the first prototype.

The final launch

On June 16th, months of work and effort were bound to come to an end. The final launch had arrived. Two projects would close their books on that day, both the MTG11b project and our seminar.

Flight #1

We started off by launching the first of the two big rockets with only a Board MPU as payload. No experiments, since if they broke on a test run, all the effort would be for nothing. The configuration was two D9-7 and four D9-P as originally planned, the projected apogee was around 265 meters above ground. After completing the final checks, we launched.

A flawless takeoff. All engines worked, the parachute deployed and the rocket was carried down to safety. The only caveat: It disappeared in the direction of a forest, never to be seen again. Stronger winds and shifting wind directions drove it so far away that we were unable to locate it, even with a dozen people and a drone searching for it for nearly one and a half hours.

But: All systems worked, and we had one remaining rocket that was identical to this one in every aspect.

Flight #2

We prepared for another launch. This time, we ignited a small rocket immediately before the big one to ensure the wind speed and direction would not ruin this one as well. Using the same engine configuration, we took off...

...and the rocket dove straight into the field next to it. An impressive apogee of maybe 2.5 meters. The reason: engine failures, multiple of them. At least two exploded, sending two more flying out of the now broken mount. The remaining two were way too weak to lift it off, sending it straight into the ground. The experiment on board did not survive the crash. The impact gave the camera MPU on board a brownout and we were unable to fix it without replacement parts we did not have.

Flight #3

The good news: We had thought of a scenario like this and brought two backup engine mounts, completely identical to the one that flew flawlessly earlier. We completed repairs, tested the engines from the same pack in isolation, and flew another small rocket for testing the wind. Then, with the last remaining experiment on board, it was time to end both projects.

This is the result of our analysis: The rocket was sent into a spiral, which was caused by - you guessed it - at least two malfunctioning engines. Hooray. The parachute did not deploy due to the engine/fin mount being lodged in place by the explosion and the rocket dove straight into the ground at more than 200km/h, which shattered the nose cone and corrupted the SD card of the experiment while destroying its MPU.

The conclusion

The final result: One destroyed rocket, one vanished rocket, two destroyed experiments with no results to show from them. If this was real science, my verdict would be that we learned a lot for the next launch and could move on to improve our designs and switch away from these terrible engines. Sadly, this is not real science and we can not work on this any longer. But: We know that our systems worked nearly perfectly. The first rocket flew without a problem, the second one would have if we had not lost the gamble against the odds behind solid propellant model rocket engines.

My verdict: Although a highly unsatisfying end, I had lots of fun and learned so much during this project that I do not regret it.
