Rocket Construction Seminar

Phase I

Designing the small rockets

The first step was having the other students build small rockets to gain familiarity with the material, workflow, simulation software and physics behind rocketry. In order to make it as easy as possible for them, we designed the rocket for them much like it had been done for us during our internship.

Components and Acquisition

We 3D-printed a custom engine/fin mount. It contains the premade mount for the rocket engine and holds the custom-made fins. The mounting points for the fins are slightly tilted to allow for a stabilizing rotation using aerodynamics.

Using the simulation software OpenRocket, we finalised the design to fly about 230m high. To power them, we opted for the same thrust source as during our internship - D9-5 class solid-propellant rocket engines. The body parts, parachute and nose cone were all ordered from Klima, a store specifically for model rocketry. To deploy the parachute, we used the engine's secondary explosive payload, which fires within 5 seconds of the primary thrust ending (this is where the '5' in D9-5 comes from).

In the simulation file, we reached about 12.2% stability, slightly below the optimum of around 15.7%. We ended up shifting the center of gravity more toward the front while finishing the build process, which updated our stability to 16%.

Appearance and Styling

For visuals, we opted for a red body, white engine/fin mount and wooden fins (custom made by the friend btw). The students were given the choice of a white or black nose cone, with the additional freedom of painting the rockets themselves, which resulted in some cool designs.

First Launch

On December 3rd, 2025, we were ready for our first launch of the project. Every single rocket had been checked, every bit of orga was done, the DLR had agreed to take care of the launching procedure and about half of our teachers had been invited to watch.

Rocket #1

The first rocket did not even lift off, but this was the result of an exploding rocket engine. A lot of these types of rocket engines fail, especially when they are not brand-new, a frustrating but inescapable caveat of solid-propellant engines (only true regarding model rocket engines, not the ones proper rockets use).

Rocket #2

Rocket 3

For the third and final launch of the day, we finally got our success...partially

As you can see, it flew flawlessly - until it was time for the parachute. Up until this point, a lot of time and care had went into making sure the rockets would fly safely and perform satisfactorily. Shortly before the launch, we realized we did not have enough fireproof material to protect the parachutes in the rockets. In a fatal short-term decision, we decided to use plastic cups to take most of the heat instead of the plastic parachutes, figuring they would just be ejected in the same way the other material would. Our decision backfired, the rocket was completely destroyed by flying straight into the ground at over 200km/h.

Our verdict

Initially, we were frustrated and annoyed at our obvious mistakes. Looking back, this is just how science works: Trying stuff, failing, trying again, failing again etc. etc. etc. - The most important part is what we figured out: Its not exactly a good idea to assume something won't get stuck :)

Hint: We also launched some of these rockets at the other two launches. After removing the stupid cup idea from the rockets, every single one flew flawlessly :D
